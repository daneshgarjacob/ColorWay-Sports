**On Thursday night, November 12, 2015, the Buffalo Bills wore all red and the New York Jets wore all green. For millions of red-green colorblind viewers, they looked like the same team.** It was the first Color Rush game, the NFL and Nike's one-color uniform promotion for Thursday Night Football, and by the next morning the league was admitting it had missed something.

Below is what happened that night, what the NFL and Nike changed, and something nobody else has done: we ran this season's 13 biggest games (every prime-time and holiday game in Weeks 11 through 13) through the same red-green colorblind filter to see whether any of them would fail.

## Quick Answers

- **What was the NFL game colorblind fans could not watch?** Bills at Jets, Thursday, Nov. 12, 2015, at MetLife Stadium. Buffalo wore all red, New York wore all green, and Buffalo won 22-17.
- **Why could colorblind fans not tell the teams apart?** Red-green color blindness is the most common kind. Red and green that look completely different to most people can look like two similar shades of olive or brown.
- **How many people are colorblind?** About 1 in 12 men, according to the National Eye Institute, and about 1 in 200 women.
- **What did the NFL say?** A league spokesman said its standard TV test had not accounted for colorblindness and that it would add a colorblindness check.
- **What changed?** In 2016, Nike brought in eye doctors from Mount Sinai Hospital in New York to flag problem matchups. The Jets wore white when they played the red Bills again.
- **Would any 2026 prime-time game fail the same test?** No. All 13 we tested pass, because every one puts one team in white. Three of them would get close to failing if both teams wore color.
- **Our take (opinion):** the white jersey is the best accessibility tool in football, and it costs nothing.

## Bills In All Red, Jets In All Green

<p style="text-align: center; font-size: 0.75em; color: #999; margin: 0.25em 0 1.5em;">Bills red and Jets green (official team color values), as most viewers see them (top) and through a red-blind protanopia filter (bottom). Graphic: ColorWay Sports</p>

Color Rush was built for Thursday Night Football in 2015: both teams in one bright color from helmet to socks, with no white jersey on the field. Four games used it that season, and Bills at Jets was the first. Buffalo wore red jerseys, red pants and red socks. New York wore green from top to bottom.

To most viewers it looked loud but clear. To red-green colorblind viewers it was a problem from the opening kickoff. CBS Sports reported that **hundreds of colorblind fans** complained they could not tell which team was which. Former NFL kicker Lawrence Tynes, who is colorblind, posted on Twitter: "If you are colorblind like me, this NFL game is going to be hard to follow." (That line was reported by FOX 5 New York and the Associated Press.) Buffalo won 22-17 in Rex Ryan's first game against the Jets since they fired him, but the uniforms were the story the next day.

## What Red-Green Colorblind Fans Actually Saw

<img src="/images/posts/nfl-game-colorblind-fans-could-not-watch-color-rush-2015/filter-2015.jpg" alt="Bills red and Jets green shown three ways: normal vision with a color difference of 59, deuteranopia with 22, and protanopia with 5" style="display: block; width: 100%; max-width: 680px; margin: 1.25em auto 0.4em;" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
<p style="text-align: center; font-size: 0.75em; color: #999; margin: 0.25em 0 1.5em;">The number on the right is the color difference (CIEDE2000; 0 means identical). Simulation and graphic: ColorWay Sports</p>

We took the two teams' official colors, Bills red (#C60C30) and Jets green (#125740), and ran them through a standard colorblind simulation. Then we measured how different the two colors are with CIEDE2000, the international (CIE) standard formula for color difference. A score of 0 means two colors are identical, and a difference of about 1 to 2 is the smallest most people can notice side by side.

| View | Bills red becomes | Jets green becomes | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Normal vision | #C60C30 | #125740 | **59** |
| Deuteranopia (green-blind) | #7D7029 | #4C4A42 | **22** |
| Protanopia (red-blind) | #524B2F | #55503F | **5** |

Through the protanopia filter, a 59-point gap collapses to 5. Both teams become a muddy olive brown, and the only difference left is a slight change in lightness. Deuteranopia keeps a little more of the gap, mostly because the Jets' green is darker than the Bills' red, but the colors that made the teams different are gone. On a TV across the room, under stadium lights, moving at full speed, that is not enough.

**How we made these:** we used the Machado, Oliveira and Fernandes (2009) model, published in the journal IEEE Transactions on Visualization and Computer Graphics, at full strength for protanopia and deuteranopia, applied to linear RGB. It is one of the most widely used simulations in color research. Full-strength simulation shows the strongest form of red-green color blindness. Many colorblind fans have a milder form, so what they saw that night falls somewhere between normal and our filtered panels.

## About 1 In 12 Men Are Colorblind

The National Eye Institute says about **1 in 12 men** have color vision deficiency, and that the most common type makes it hard to tell red from green. The National Library of Medicine's MedlinePlus puts red-green color vision deficiency at about 1 in 12 men and **1 in 200 women** among people of Northern European ancestry, and lower in most other groups. It is far more common in men because the genes for red and green light sensors sit on the X chromosome.

For a national TV audience, that is not a rounding error. In 2016, ESPN's Darren Rovell cited Nike's figure of roughly **10 million Americans** who are colorblind.

## The NFL Said Its TV Test Missed It

The next day, NFL spokesman Brian McCarthy told the Associated Press in an email that the league's standard television test had not accounted for colorblind fans watching at home, and that the NFL would add a colorblindness analysis to its testing. CBS Sports reported the league had tested the uniforms on the field and on TV over the summer. The problem was not that nobody looked. It was that nobody looked through the eyes of a colorblind viewer.

## What Changed In 2016

Color Rush expanded to every team in 2016, which meant many more color-against-color games. This time Nike brought in doctors from Mount Sinai Hospital in New York to flag the matchups that would be a problem for colorblind viewers, according to ESPN. The flagged teams wore white instead:

| 2016 matchup | The problem | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Jets at Bills, Sept. 15 | Red vs. green, the 2015 game again | Jets wore all white |
| Browns vs. Ravens | Brown would blend with purple | Browns wore white |
| Rams vs. Seahawks | Yellow vs. green | Rams wore white |

The Jets announced the look as "All. White." on Twitter, and a team executive told reporters they kept the white facemask, gloves, shoes and socks to stay in the spirit of the program. We checked the Rams-Seahawks call with our own filter, using today's Rams yellow and Seattle's Action Green: they score 29 apart for most viewers and only **7** through the protanopia filter. Nike's doctors were right.

## Our 2026 Test: The 13 Biggest Games Of Weeks 11-13

Here is the part nobody else has. We took every prime-time and holiday game from Week 11 through Week 13 of the 2026 season, used the uniforms on each team's published schedule (from our [2026 NFL uniform schedule](/stories/nfl-uniform-schedule-2026)), and sampled each jersey's body color from the jersey images we use on the site. Then we ran every pair through both filters.

Our line: a difference **under 10** through either filter is risky, 10 to 20 is one to watch, and over 20 is clear.

**All 13 pass, and it is not close.** The lowest scores belong to two light-on-light games, Broncos white against the Steelers' gold throwback on Black Friday and Chiefs white against the Rams' yellow Classic Sol on Thursday, Dec. 3, at 34 each. Those numbers barely move through the filters, because white and yellow-gold stay apart for red-green colorblind fans; the gap there is the same for every viewer.

The reason is simple. Every one of the 13 games has one team in white. White does not change through a red-green filter, and it sits far from almost any team color in lightness. Across all 42 games in Weeks 11 through 13, by our count of the team schedules, every game has at least one team in a white or white-based jersey (the Buccaneers' creamsicle throwback on Dec. 6 is the white '76 jersey, not the orange one).

## Our Take

Color Rush looked great to most people, and that was the problem: nobody tested it for the 1 in 12 men who do not see red and green the way the uniform designers did. The NFL fixed it the cheapest way possible, by putting one team back in white, and that is still the fix. Our test of this season's biggest games found nothing to worry about, but only because of the white jersey. Every time the league adds another color-against-color game, someone should run it through a filter first. We will keep doing it for every prime-time game.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What NFL game could colorblind fans not watch?**

The Buffalo Bills at the New York Jets on Thursday, November 12, 2015, at MetLife Stadium. The Bills wore all red and the Jets wore all green in the first Color Rush game, and many red-green colorblind viewers could not tell the teams apart.

**Who won the Bills-Jets Color Rush game?**

The Bills, 22-17. It was Rex Ryan's first game against the Jets as Buffalo's coach, after six seasons coaching New York.

**What was Color Rush?**

An NFL and Nike promotion that put both teams in one bright color, head to toe, for Thursday Night Football. It started with four games in 2015 and expanded to every team in 2016.

**Why do red and green look the same to colorblind people?**

The most common kind of color blindness affects the cone cells that sense red or green light. With protanopia (red-blind) or deuteranopia (green-blind), red and green shift toward similar yellows, olives and browns, and the main difference left is how light or dark they are.

**How many people are red-green colorblind?**

About 1 in 12 men have color vision deficiency, according to the National Eye Institute, and red-green is the most common type. MedlinePlus puts it at about 1 in 200 women. Rates are highest in people of Northern European ancestry.

**What did the NFL say after the Bills-Jets game?**

NFL spokesman Brian McCarthy told the Associated Press that the league's standard TV test had not accounted for colorblind fans at home and that the NFL would add a colorblindness analysis to its testing.

**Did the Jets wear green against the Bills again?**

No. When the teams met in a Color Rush game on September 15, 2016, in Buffalo, the Bills wore red again and the Jets wore all white after doctors flagged the red-green matchup.

**Which other Color Rush matchups were changed for colorblind fans?**

According to ESPN, Nike's consultants from Mount Sinai flagged Browns vs. Ravens (brown and purple) and Rams vs. Seahawks (yellow and green), and the Browns and Rams wore white.

**Are any 2026 NFL prime-time games bad for colorblind fans?**

Not by our test. All 13 prime-time and holiday games in Weeks 11 through 13 pass because each has one team in white. The closest are two light-on-light games, Broncos at Steelers on Black Friday and Chiefs at Rams on December 3, and those gaps are the same for colorblind and non-colorblind viewers.

**How did you test the uniforms?**

We sampled each jersey's main color, ran it through the Machado (2009) colorblind simulation for protanopia and deuteranopia, and scored the difference between the two teams with CIEDE2000, a standard color-difference formula. Under 10 is our risky line.

**Does the NFL still test uniforms for colorblindness?**

The league said in 2015 it would add a colorblindness analysis, and Nike used eye doctors to check the 2016 Color Rush matchups. We have not found a public statement describing the league's current process.

**Can colorblind fans tell white from a team color?**

Almost always. White does not change through a red-green filter, and it is much lighter than nearly every team color, which is why one white jersey solves the problem.

**What is the difference between protanopia and deuteranopia?**

Protanopia means the red-sensing cones do not work, so reds also look darker. Deuteranopia means the green-sensing cones do not work. Both make red and green hard to tell apart. Milder versions, protanomaly and deuteranomaly, are more common.

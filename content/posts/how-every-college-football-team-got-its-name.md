College nicknames are weirder than the pros', mostly because nobody planned them. They came from student editors, sportswriters on deadline, a coach talking to a crowd at a train depot and, more than once, an insult that a school decided to keep. Oklahoma is named after people who cheated in a land race. Purdue is named after a rival's put-down. Virginia Tech's name is a word a student made up to win five dollars. And Indiana's name is a word nobody, including the state of Indiana, can define.

So we went back to the start for all 68 Power 4 programs, the SEC, Big Ten, Big 12 and ACC, plus Notre Dame. We used each school's own athletics history, university archives and libraries, state historical societies and encyclopedias, and newspaper research. Where the famous story is shaky or the record disagrees with it, we say so.

This is part five of our team-name series, after [How Every NFL Team Got Its Name](/stories/how-every-nfl-team-got-its-name), [MLB](/stories/how-every-mlb-team-got-its-name), [NBA](/stories/how-every-nba-team-got-its-name) and [NHL](/stories/how-every-nhl-team-got-its-name).

## Quick Facts: College Football Team Names

- **Among the oldest:** Purdue's Boilermakers (1891), Auburn's Tigers (1892), Cal's Golden Bears and Iowa State's Cyclones (both 1895), and LSU and Clemson (both 1896).
- **Most recent big change:** the Syracuse Orange, which became the name for every Syracuse team in 2004. Stanford became the singular "Cardinal" in 1981.
- **Named by a newspaper:** Iowa State (a Chicago Tribune headline), USC (an LA Times sports editor), Arizona (an LA Times game story), Northwestern (a Chicago Tribune reporter), Nebraska (a Lincoln sportswriter), Michigan State (a Lansing sports editor), Georgia (two Atlanta columnists) and Tennessee (an unsigned Atlanta Constitution story).
- **Named by students:** Texas, Duke and Wake Forest (student editors), Baylor, Colorado, SMU, UCF, Florida State and Arizona State (student contests or votes).
- **Started as an insult:** Sooners (land-rush cheats), Boilermakers (a rival's jab), Badgers (miners living in holes) and, most likely, Tar Heels.
- **Named after a color:** the Stanford Cardinal and the Syracuse Orange. Not the bird, not the fruit.
- **The $5 club:** Virginia Tech's "Hokie" won a $5 cheer prize, Baylor's bear won a $5 gold piece and Colorado's "Buffaloes" won a $5 contest.
- **One coach, two names:** John Bender named the Kansas State Wildcats in 1915 and the Houston Cougars in 1927.
- **Nobody knows for sure:** Hoosier, Wolverine and the exact start of the Fighting Irish.

## SEC

### Alabama Crimson Tide

Before "Crimson Tide," Alabama's teams were the Varsity, the Crimson White and, most popularly, the Thin Red Line. The university credits Hugh Roberts, a former sports editor of the Birmingham Age-Herald, with coining "Crimson Tide" for the 1907 Alabama-Auburn game in Birmingham, a muddy 6-6 tie in which Alabama was the big underdog. Zipp Newman of the Birmingham News did the most to popularize it after 1919. The Roberts story is shakier than it sounds, though. Alabama's own history hedges, and when NCAA.com tracked down part of Roberts' 1907 recap in 2022, neither "crimson" nor "tide" was in it. The earliest "Crimson Tide" NCAA.com found in print was from 1914. One more footnote: that 1907 game was the last Alabama-Auburn meeting until 1948. ### Arkansas Razorbacks

Arkansas teams were the Cardinals until 1909. That November, coach Hugo Bezdek's team beat LSU 16-0 in Memphis to finish 7-0. When the train got back to Fayetteville, Bezdek told the crowd at the depot that his players had played "like a wild band of Razorback hogs." Students voted to change the mascot from Cardinals to Razorbacks starting the next school term, and Arkansas has played as the Razorbacks since 1910. ### Auburn Tigers

Auburn says its teams have been the Tigers since the very first game, against Georgia in Atlanta in 1892. The name comes from a poem. Oliver Goldsmith's "The Deserted Village" (1770) has a line about crouching tigers waiting for their prey, and the town itself took its name from the same poem, which opens "Sweet Auburn, loveliest village of the plain." That line also gave Auburn its old "Plainsmen" label. To settle the bar argument: "War Eagle" is the battle cry, not the nickname. Auburn says it has only one nickname, the Tigers, and has never called its teams the Eagles. [Auburn uniform schedule 2026](/stories/auburn-uniform-schedule-2026)

### Florida Gators

Florida's name came from a pennant order. Austin Miller told the story in a 1948 interview that UF Athletics still reprints. In 1907 he was a law student at Virginia, and his father Phillip, who ran a drug and stationery store in Gainesville, visited and ordered University of Florida pennants from a Charlottesville company. The manager asked what Florida's emblem was. There wasn't one. Austin suggested the alligator, because it was native to Florida and no other school used it. The manager had never seen an alligator, so Miller found a picture in the UVA library for the designers. The blue pennants with an orange gator went up in the Millers' store in 1908, and the name caught on. Many retellings say the trip was in 1908; Miller said 1907. ### Georgia Bulldogs

Old-timers credit Yale, the original Bulldogs. Georgia's first president, Abraham Baldwin, was a Yale man, and early campus buildings were modeled on Yale's. But that is tradition. The paper trail starts in 1920, when the team was mostly called the Red and Black. On Nov. 3, 1920, Morgan Blake of the Atlanta Journal pitched "The Georgia Bulldogs," writing that "there is a certain dignity about a bulldog, as well as ferocity." After a 0-0 tie at Virginia three days later, Cliff Wheatley of the Atlanta Constitution used "Bulldogs" five times in his game story, and it stuck. ### Kentucky Wildcats

On Oct. 9, 1909, Kentucky, then called State University, won 6-2 at Illinois. Back on campus, Commandant Carbusier, head of the school's military department, told students at a chapel service that the team had "fought like Wildcats." Fans and newspapers ran with it, and the university adopted it. The costumed Wildcat mascot is much younger than the name: UK dates it to the 1976-77 season. ### LSU Tigers

LSU took the name in the fall of 1896, when coach A.W. Jeardeau's team went 6-0. LSU says the name goes back to the Civil War "Louisiana Tigers." That nickname started with the Tiger Rifles, a New Orleans volunteer company in Zouave uniforms, then spread to its battalion and eventually to all Louisiana troops in Lee's Army of Northern Virginia. LSU's first postwar president, David French Boyd, had served with those troops, and in the program's fourth season the teams took the name for themselves. ### Ole Miss Rebels

"Ole Miss" came first, from an 1896 contest to name the new yearbook, won by Elma Meek of Oxford. The teams went through several names, including a stretch as "the Flood" before 1936. That year the student paper ran a new contest that drew more than 200 entries. Five finalists went to Southern sportswriters, and 18 of the 21 who answered picked "Rebels," which had been suggested by Judge Ben Guider of Vicksburg. The athletic committee chairman, Judge William Hemingway, signed off with a line Ole Miss still quotes: "If 18 sports writers wish to use 'Rebels,' I shall not rebel." ### Mississippi State Bulldogs

The first Mississippi A&M teams were the Aggies. After the school became Mississippi State College in 1932, they were mostly the Maroons, for the uniform color. That year cadets celebrated an 11-0 win over Ole Miss in Jackson with a mock funeral down Capitol Street, a bulldog pup riding on a coffin draped in Ole Miss colors. The first live Bully was killed by a campus bus in 1939 and buried under the bench at the 50-yard line. [Mississippi State uniform schedule 2026](/stories/mississippi-state-uniform-schedule-2026)

### Missouri Tigers

Mizzou ties its name to the Civil War. When a rumor spread that Bill Anderson's guerrilla band was coming to sack Columbia, townspeople formed an armed guard, built a blockhouse and fortified the old courthouse. The company was called the "Missouri Tigers." The raiders never came. Soon after Missouri fielded its first football team in 1890, the athletic committee adopted "Tigers" in honor of those defenders. The mascot got his own name, Truman, after Harry S. Truman, in 1984. [Missouri uniform schedule 2026](/stories/missouri-uniform-schedule-2026)

### Oklahoma Sooners

Here is the land grab. A "Sooner" was someone who slipped into the Unassigned Lands before the official start of the 1889 Land Run to grab the best claims early. The law that opened the land, passed March 2, 1889, specifically denied land to anyone who entered too soon, and settlers thought very little of sooners. OU's teams were the Rough Riders or the Boomers before "Sooners" took over in 1908. The Encyclopedia of Oklahoma History says the word was already losing its sting by then, and by the 1920s it was a badge of pride. One more twist: the "Sooner State" nickname apparently was never made official by the state. [Oklahoma uniform schedule 2026](/stories/oklahoma-uniform-schedule-2026)

### South Carolina Gamecocks

South Carolina's archivist traces the name to a near-riot. In 1902, after Carolina upset Clemson during State Fair week, students paraded a drawing of a gamecock crowing over a beaten tiger. Clemson cadets marched on campus to take it, about 40 armed Carolina students met them, and police and professors talked both sides into burning the drawing together. The schools stopped playing for six years, and newspapers started calling Carolina the Gamecocks. Why a gamecock: Revolutionary War general Thomas Sumter was nicknamed "the Gamecock." Who printed the name first is disputed. Some credit The State newspaper in 1903, others the student paper. [South Carolina uniform schedule 2026](/stories/south-carolina-uniform-schedule-2026)

### Tennessee Volunteers

Tennessee was the Volunteer State long before it had a football team. The name came out of the War of 1812 and stuck during the Mexican-American War, when the governor asked for 2,600 volunteers and more than 30,000 answered. UT's teams were mostly "the Tennesseans" or "the Varsity" until 1902, when they beat Georgia Tech 10-6 on a late touchdown and the next morning's Atlanta Constitution called them the "Volunteers." The story had no byline, so nobody knows who wrote it. Knoxville's papers used the name regularly from 1905. [Tennessee uniform schedule 2026](/stories/tennessee-uniform-schedule-2026)

### Texas Longhorns

Student editors willed this one into existence. A month into the 1903 fall term, Alex Weisberg, editor of the student paper The Texan, told sports reporter D.A. Frank to call UT's teams "Longhorns" in every story, "and we'll soon have a name." It worked. UT historian Jim Nicar says that by 1907 the name was in campus-wide use and the Athletic Council recognized the Longhorn as the mascot. Texas students did not invent the term, though: researcher Barry Popik found "long horns" used for Texas in Galveston and San Antonio papers in 1900. And after Texas won at Oklahoma in November 1903, the Daily Oklahoman tried to call them the "Texas Rangers." [Texas uniform schedule 2026](/stories/texas-uniform-schedule-2026)

### Texas A&M Aggies

The school opened in 1876 as the Agricultural and Mechanical College of Texas, and in the early 1900s its students were called "Farmers." Texas A&M says "Aggie" first came along in the 1920s, and it became the official student-body nickname in 1949, when the yearbook changed its name to "Aggieland." ### Indiana Hoosiers

### Wisconsin Badgers

Wisconsin took the state's nickname, and the state got it from lead miners. During the mining boom of the 1820s and 1830s in southwest Wisconsin, the Wisconsin Blue Book says, miners too busy digging to build houses moved into old mine shafts and makeshift burrows, like badgers. It started as an insult. One historian called the evidence "ludicrously tenuous," but the Wisconsin Historical Society backs the mining story. Bucky Badger arrived in 1949, and the badger did not become the state animal until 1957. [Wisconsin uniform schedule 2026](/stories/wisconsin-uniform-schedule-2026)

### Kansas Jayhawks

"Jayhawker" came out of the 1850s "Bleeding Kansas" era, when it was used for free-state fighters, like James Lane's men, battling proslavery forces, and later for Kansas troops in general. KU says the name has a double meaning, since raiders on both sides were called jayhawkers. Students adopted the bird in the 1880s, it was in the Rock Chalk chant by 1886, and the first football team in 1890 was the Jayhawkers. Where the word itself came from is disputed. The Kansas Historical Society lists three theories: that it is older than Kansas and of unknown origin, that a Free-State Irishman named Pat Devlin coined "jayhawking" for his raids in 1856, or that the Jayhawk is a mythical cross between a blue jay and a sparrow hawk. The bird got its shoes in 1912, "for kicking opponents." [Kansas uniform schedule 2026](/stories/kansas-uniform-schedule-2026)

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Why are they called the Oklahoma Sooners?**

A "Sooner" was someone who sneaked into the Unassigned Lands before the official start of the 1889 Land Run to claim land early, which the law prohibited. It was an insult at first. OU adopted the name in 1908, and by the 1920s it had become a point of pride.

**Why is Alabama called the Crimson Tide?**

Alabama credits Birmingham sportswriter Hugh Roberts, who supposedly used it for the 1907 Alabama-Auburn game, and Zipp Newman of the Birmingham News for popularizing it. Researchers have not found "Crimson Tide" in Roberts' 1907 recap, and the earliest confirmed print use is from 1914, so the exact start is uncertain.

**What is a Hoosier?**

Nobody knows for sure. The Indiana Historical Bureau says the best-supported theory is that it was Southern slang for a rough countryman. The earliest known written use is from 1831. Stories about "Who's here?" are considered folk tales.

**What is a Hokie?**

It is a made-up word. Virginia Tech student O.M. Stull invented it in 1896 for the "Old Hokie" cheer, which won a $5 contest after the school changed its name.

**Why is North Carolina called the Tar Heels?**

The name comes from North Carolina's tar, pitch and turpentine industry. It was used for North Carolina soldiers by 1863, probably first as an insult from other states' troops, and North Carolinians turned it into a boast.

**What is a Boilermaker, and why is Purdue called that?**

After Purdue beat Wabash 44-0 in 1891, a Crawfordsville newspaper called the Purdue players "Burly Boiler Makers," a jab at the engineering school's tough, ringer-looking team. Purdue kept it.

**Why is Notre Dame called the Fighting Irish?**

There is no single documented origin. Notre Dame says the most accepted explanation is that the press coined it for the team's fighting spirit, probably as a taunt at first. University president Rev. Matthew Walsh made it official in 1927.

**Why is Stanford the Cardinal and not the Cardinals?**

Because it is the color, not the bird. Stanford dropped its Indian mascot in 1972 and used "Cardinals" for the color until Nov. 17, 1981, when President Donald Kennedy made it the singular "Cardinal."

**What is a Jayhawk?**

It is a mythical bird. "Jayhawker" was a name for free-state fighters in 1850s Kansas, and KU students adopted the bird in the 1880s. Where the word came from is disputed; one theory says it combines the blue jay and the sparrow hawk.

**Why is Ohio State called the Buckeyes?**

Ohio is the Buckeye State, named for the Ohio buckeye tree, whose nut looks like a deer's eye. Ohio State made "Buckeyes" its official nickname in 1950.

**Why is Duke called the Blue Devils?**

Student editors started using it in 1922 for the French Chasseurs Alpins, World War I mountain troops in blue uniforms known as "les Diables Bleus." The school was still Trinity College at the time.

**What is a Nittany Lion?**

A mountain lion from Mount Nittany, next to Penn State's campus. Third baseman Joe Mason invented it on a 1904 baseball trip to Princeton to answer the Princeton Tiger.

**What is a horned frog?**

It is not a frog. TCU's Horned Frog is the Texas horned lizard, and the school has used the name since 1897.

**Why is Michigan called the Wolverines?**

Nobody knows for sure. The main theories are the 1830s Toledo War with Ohio, the fur trade, and an insult aimed at settlers.

**Why is Syracuse called the Orange?**

Syracuse chose orange as its color in 1890 because no other college used it alone. Its teams were the Orange or Orangemen, and in 2004 every team became the Syracuse Orange.

**Why is Texas called the Longhorns?**

In 1903 the editor of the student paper told its sports reporter to call the teams "Longhorns" in every story until the name stuck. By 1907 the Athletic Council had recognized the Longhorn.

**How did the Florida Gators get their name?**

In 1907, Gainesville merchant Phillip Miller and his son Austin ordered Florida pennants in Charlottesville, Va. The school had no emblem, so Austin suggested the alligator. The pennants went on sale in 1908.

**Why is Iowa State called the Cyclones?**

After Iowa State beat Northwestern 36-0 in 1895, the Chicago Tribune ran the headline "Struck by a Cyclone." The name stuck right away.

**Which college football nicknames started as insults?**

Oklahoma's Sooners, Purdue's Boilermakers and Wisconsin's Badgers all started as insults, and North Carolina's Tar Heels most likely did too.

**Which college team names came from a newspaper?**

Iowa State, USC, Arizona, Northwestern, Nebraska, Michigan State, Georgia and Tennessee all got their names from newspaper writers or headlines.

## The Bottom Line

Pro teams pick names. College teams mostly got stuck with them, from sportswriters on deadline, students with a $5 prize on the line and rivals trying to be mean. That is why so many of them are still a little strange: a Hokie that means nothing, a Horned Frog that is a lizard, a Cardinal that is a color and a Sooner that used to mean a cheater. And it is why so many college uniforms look the way they do, from Florida's orange-and-blue gator to Minnesota's all-gold look and Syracuse's single color.
